Progressive waves

Phase and phase difference

Move point B along the wave and watch how its motion compares with A. Switch to longitudinal to see the same idea with particles oscillating along the direction of travel.

wave travels →
Δx
Δφ (degrees)
Δφ (radians)

Key equations

Δφ = 2πΔx / λΔφ = 360° × Δx / λv = fλ

Breaking it down

  1. Phase is where a particle is in its cycle of oscillation. Phase difference is how far one point is ahead of or behind another, in degrees or radians.
  2. On a progressive wave the phase difference depends only on how the separation compares with the wavelength: one whole λ is one whole cycle (2π rad).
  3. A point further along the direction of travel lags. The wave reaches it later, so it does the same thing a fraction of a period later.
  4. Phase difference is quoted between 0 and 2π. A separation of 1.25λ gives 2.5π rad, which is the same as 0.5π rad.
  5. Particles oscillate about fixed positions. Energy moves along the wave, the medium does not. In a longitudinal wave the oscillation is parallel to the direction of travel.

Common traps

  • Calculator in degrees when your answer is in radians (or the other way round).
  • Crest to the next trough is λ/2, so they are in antiphase (π rad), not λ apart.
  • In a longitudinal wave, the centre of a compression is a point of zero displacement, not maximum displacement.
  • Saying particles "move along with the wave". They don't.

Try this: set the separation to 0.50λ, then 1.00λ, then 1.50λ. Which settings put A and B in antiphase?

Superposition

Key equations

f₁ = (1/2L)√(T/μ)v = √(T/μ)λₙ = 2L/nfₙ = nf₁

Breaking it down

  1. A wave travels to the fixed end and reflects. The incident and reflected waves have the same frequency, wavelength and (roughly) the same amplitude, and travel in opposite directions.
  2. They superpose. At some points they always cancel: these are nodes (zero amplitude). The fixed ends must be nodes.
  3. Halfway between nodes the waves always reinforce: antinodes (maximum amplitude). Node to adjacent node is λ/2.
  4. All points between two adjacent nodes move in phase. Points in adjacent loops are in antiphase. Amplitude changes with position.
  5. Only frequencies that fit a whole number of loops on the string are possible. The nth harmonic has n loops, so L = nλ/2.
  6. A stationary wave stores energy but transfers no net energy along the string.

Common traps

  • Node to node is λ/2, not λ. Two successive minima of a microwave or sound stationary wave are also λ/2 apart.
  • The "first harmonic" is the fundamental. The second harmonic has two loops.
  • μ must be in kg m⁻¹. Convert g m⁻¹ by dividing by 1000.
  • Quadrupling T only doubles f, because f ∝ √T.

Try this: note f₁, then double the tension. By what factor does f₁ change? Now halve L instead.

Interference and coherence

Light from two slits spreads out and overlaps. The left view shows the waves superposing (exaggerated scale). The right strip shows the real fringe pattern on a screen for the values you set.

field view not to scale · screen to scale
fringe spacing w
w = λD / s

Key equations

w = λD / sbright: path diff = nλdark: path diff = (n + ½)λ

Breaking it down

  1. Coherent sources have the same frequency and a constant phase difference. Without coherence the pattern moves about too fast to see.
  2. At any point on the screen, compare the distances to each slit. This path difference decides the result.
  3. Path difference of a whole number of wavelengths → waves arrive in phase → constructive → bright fringe.
  4. Path difference of an odd number of half-wavelengths → antiphase → destructive → dark fringe.
  5. Fringe spacing w = λD/s holds when D is much bigger than s. Measure across many fringes and divide, to cut the percentage uncertainty.
  6. Using a laser: monochromatic and coherent. Using white light: central fringe white, the others are spectra with blue closest to the centre.

Common traps

  • s is the slit separation (centre to centre), not the slit width.
  • Mixed units: s in mm, w in mm, D in m, λ in nm. Convert everything to metres.
  • Coherent does not mean "in phase". It means a constant phase difference.
  • Laser safety: never look into the beam, avoid shiny surfaces that reflect it, display warning signs.

Single slit and grating

Diffraction

Switch between a single slit (intensity pattern on a screen 2.0 m away) and a diffraction grating (angles of each order). Try white light on the grating to see the spectra overlap.

screen 2.0 m from slit

Key equations

d sin θ = nλd = 1 / (lines per metre)nmax = d/λ, rounded down

Breaking it down

  1. Single slit: diffraction is most noticeable when the slit width is about the same as λ. The central maximum is twice as wide as the others and much brighter.
  2. A narrower slit or a longer wavelength spreads the pattern out: the central maximum gets wider. A narrower slit also makes it dimmer.
  3. Grating: θ is measured from the straight-through (zero-order) beam. If you are given the angle between two matching orders on either side, halve it.
  4. Find d from lines per mm: 300 lines mm⁻¹ → d = 1/300 mm = 3.33 × 10⁻⁶ m.
  5. sin θ cannot exceed 1, so nmax is d/λ rounded down. Total spots seen = 2nmax + 1.
  6. More slits make the maxima sharper and brighter, which is why gratings are used for accurate wavelength measurements and spectroscopy.

Common traps

  • Rounding nmax up. 5.66 means the 5th order is the highest.
  • Using lines per mm directly as d.
  • With white light, the zero order is white because every wavelength has zero path difference at θ = 0.
  • If d/λ is exactly a whole number, that last order would be at 90° and is not observed.

Try this: on the grating with white light at 500 lines mm⁻¹, look for where the second and third orders overlap.

Refraction, TIR and optical fibres

Change the angle and refractive indices to find the critical angle. Switch to the fibre view to see when light stays trapped in the core.

ray from medium 1 into medium 2

Key equations

n = c / csn₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂sin θc = n₂ / n₁

Breaking it down

  1. All angles are measured from the normal. Light slows down in a higher-n medium and bends towards the normal.
  2. Total internal reflection needs two things: light going from higher n to lower n, and an angle of incidence greater than the critical angle.
  3. An optical fibre has a high-n core and lower-n cladding. Cladding keeps TIR at the core boundary, protects the core from scratches that would let light escape, and stops signals crossing between fibres.
  4. Modal dispersion: rays taking different paths arrive at different times. Reduce it with a very narrow (monomode) core.
  5. Material dispersion: different wavelengths travel at different speeds in glass. Reduce it with a monochromatic source.
  6. Both cause pulse broadening: pulses can overlap, which limits the data rate. Absorption reduces amplitude. Regenerators (repeaters) restore the signal.

Common traps

  • Measuring angles from the surface instead of the normal.
  • sin θc = n₂/n₁ is always smaller ÷ larger. If you get a value above 1, there is no critical angle.
  • Mixing up modal and material dispersion and their fixes.

Try this: in the fibre view, raise the cladding index towards the core index. What happens to the largest entry angle that stays trapped?

Transverse waves

Polarisation

Unpolarised light passes through a fixed polariser, then an analyser you can rotate. The graph shows how the transmitted intensity changes as you turn it.

source → polariser → analyser → detector
angle between axes
intensity after analyser

Key idea

Only transverse waves can be polarised.

Breaking it down

  1. In unpolarised light, the oscillations are in every plane perpendicular to the direction of travel.
  2. A polariser lets through only oscillations in one plane. The light is now plane polarised.
  3. Rotating a second filter (the analyser) through 360°: maximum at 0°, zero at 90°, maximum at 180°, zero at 270°. Two maxima and two minima per turn.
  4. Light can be polarised, so it must be a transverse wave. Sound cannot be polarised because it is longitudinal.
  5. Uses: polaroid sunglasses cut glare, because light reflected off water or roads is partly polarised. TV and radio aerials are lined up with the transmitter's plane of polarisation.

Common traps

  • Intensity is zero at 90°, not 180°. At 180° the axes are parallel again.
  • A metal grille for microwaves absorbs oscillations parallel to its rods. Maximum signal gets through when the rods are perpendicular to the plane of oscillation.
  • For AQA you need the pattern only. The cos² relationship drawn on the graph is shown for interest.
